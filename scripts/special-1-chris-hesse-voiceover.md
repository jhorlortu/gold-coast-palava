## 1. Cold open · 0:00

At eleven forty-seven on a Saturday night, long after we had all gone home, my phone lit up with a message from Aba, The Restless, in the team's WhatsApp group: she had just finished The Eyes of Ghana, the documentary about Nkrumah's cameraman that IShowSpeed put on his YouTube channel, free, on the seventeenth of November, and she wanted to know who this Chris Hesse really was, and why she was only now hearing his name.

My people, what dey go on? I am Kofi, the Editor, and this is a Gold Coast Palava Special: no front page tonight, no sections, just one life, a handful of honest questions, and the chat that kept four of us awake until nearly two in the morning. The film is directed by Ben Proudfoot and backed by Barack and Michelle Obama, and if you have the data, watch it; tonight, we look at its subject with the eyes of Ghana.

## 2. Question one: how does a boy from OH-soo end up behind the camera? · 0:45

Baaba, The Ascendant, who rarely types more than a line, asked the first question properly: how does a boy from OH-soo, born on the twenty-ninth of August 1932 to a Presbyterian priest, end up holding the camera at the birth of a nation? I knew why she asked, because in her house, as in many of ours, the respectable roads are medicine, law, pharmacy and engineering, and everything else is a detour. Christian CHWEE Hesse grew up in a home where the cinema was forbidden, so he did what curious boys do and sneaked out to watch Charlie Chaplin in the dark.

In 1954, the year he finished school, the colonial government's West African film school at KAHN-dah, on the very ground where TV3 stands today, was training Africans to make the colonial state's films, and the young man walked in, chose the camera department on purpose, and later completed his training at the national film school in WOOTCH, Poland, in 1960. Then came the part I call positioning: by his own account he filmed Nkrumah walking out of James Fort prison in 1951 from the roof of a van, he stood behind Camera Two on Independence Day, the sixth of March 1957, and he kept turning up with the best angle until Nkrumah noticed "this chap" and asked the junior officer how much he was paid; soon a letter arrived whose first line said the leader "has commanded", lifting him straight into the senior grade as the personal cameraman.

His father's verdict was a question, you are going to make films for what, yet the same father later went to the airport with his mother to see, with his own eyes, that his son really had come back from America. Intentionality, plus skill, plus opportunity, equals transformation: the journey only makes sense later, but somebody has to choose it first.

## 3. Question two: witness or believer? · 2:30

Kwame, The Combative, did not even wait for a full stop: so the man was Nkrumah's propaganda unit, abeg, why are we calling it history? E no be small question, and the film does not dodge it. Hesse loved Nkrumah and says so freely, and they shared a private code: Nkrumah's walking stick, carried not for a bad leg but to tell his cameraman when to roll and when to stop. That is a believer.

But on the first of August 1962, at koo-loong-GOO-goo, a border village in the Upper East, Hesse was standing in the middle of the crowd, filming schoolchildren performing for the President, when a bomb went off; he has said that the aide-de-camp, Captain Buckman, threw Nkrumah down and covered him with his own body, and children died that day while the camera was rolling. That is a witness. The same man went on to film Ankrah, Afrifa, Busia and Rawlings, governments that agreed on very little, which is not what a party propagandist does.

The film is fair enough to let the other side speak. Anita Afonu, the filmmaker who walks us through the story, reminds us that her history books and her own mother remember Nkrumah as intolerant of dissent, and an archive report in the film says at least a thousand opponents were jailed; Hesse insists America organised the 1966 coup, and a coup leader insists, on camera, that nobody helped them. When a student asks Hesse to prove that Nkrumah was a good man, he refuses: he cannot prove whether Nkrumah was an angel or Satan, he only filmed him, and it is for you to watch and judge. Kwame read that, typed "hmm", and went quiet, which, for Kwame, is a concession speech.

## 4. Question three: who owns a nation's memory? · 4:20

Aba came back with the question that cracked it open: if the coup makers burned everything, how are we even watching these films? Here is the one per cent: Ghana had no laboratory for colour film, and the heat was hard on film anyway, so every colour reel shot here was flown to a laboratory in London, which sent prints back to Accra and kept the original negatives in cold vaults.

On Thursday the twenty-fourth of February 1966, Nkrumah was travelling to Hanoi, with Hesse in the delegation, when the soldiers took Accra. On the flight on to Moscow, Hesse remembers, the men around the President drifted off to chat and laugh among themselves, so Nkrumah drew a curtain, came to the corner where his cameraman sat with the equipment, and told him that coups would go on across Africa until a new Africa germinated from the ashes: don't be afraid, keep on filming. In Moscow a Kremlin car drove Nkrumah away, and Hesse never saw him again. At home, the National Liberation Council ordered the old regime's records, books and films destroyed, and they burned what they could find, but nobody had told them about the negatives in London. The black-and-white films, which stayed at home, were not so lucky: when the film corporation was sold in the nineties to the Malaysian company behind TV3, Hesse says, the black-and-white archive was thrown away.

Surviving was only the beginning. The film says the cans were once thrown out into a car park; Hesse says the storage fees once fell nearly three years behind, until a laboratory near London agreed to restore the collection frame by frame. He spent some twenty-one years asking government after government to digitise more than a thousand reels, and each met the same dilemma: why pay for old film when you must build hospitals and schools? His reply was simple: they belong to Mother Ghana. Only now, after the film, has the government committed money, through its twenty-four-hour economy programme, to digitise the films held in Britain, and the work is said to be underway.

## 5. Question four: why did it take outsiders? · 6:10

Then Baaba asked the one that stung: why did it take a Canadian director, a former American president and a streamer from Ohio to put a Ghanaian in the spotlight? Fair, but Ghanaians made this film too: Anita Afonu and Yaw Amponsah, both trained at our own film institute, are among its producers, and Afonu admits that in film school they talked about Hesse but never taught about him. The lesson is not that foreigners care more; it is that a story nobody keeps is a story nobody can tell.

## 6. The lesson · 6:45

Close to two o'clock, Aba asked me for the lesson, so here it is. Today he is ninety-four, and in the film this man whose whole life was his eyes sits before an eye chart, struggles with the letters, and tells the camera he has glaucoma but refuses to accept that he will be blind, because there is more work to be done while we are alive. Choose your road on purpose, even when your father asks what it is for; learn your craft until your angle is the one they send for; and document everything and back it up, because what looks ordinary on your phone tonight may be the only record of our time tomorrow. Chale, that man kept the light burning for sixty years with tins of film; you carry a camera and cloud storage in your pocket, so no excuse. And for Aba, who still talks about leaving, he left a line at the end of a long interview with Kafui Dey this April: Ghana is what we have; you can live in London or anywhere else, but you do not belong there.
