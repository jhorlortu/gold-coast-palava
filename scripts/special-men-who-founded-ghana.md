### 2. QUESTION ONE · 0:50
**Pinned card: "What was here before them?"**

> **10:21 PM · Amo 🎙️:** before we fight about two men… what was the Gold Coast like BEFORE them?
> **10:22 PM · Aba:** Amo always taking us back to the beginning 😅

*[B-roll: Christiansborg Castle; 1890s coastal towns; an Aborigines' Rights Protection Society document; Casely Hayford (archival, credited); Gold Coast Regiment soldiers in Burma; 1948 boycott headlines. Dateline in typewriter type: "CAPE COAST · 4 AUGUST 1897".]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** Amo, The Discerning, who dictates his messages, asked us to start where most of these arguments never start: before either man. The Gold Coast of the 1940s was a British colony ruled from Christiansborg Castle, and resistance did not begin with a political party. On the fourth of August 1897, lawyers and chiefs on the coast formed the Aborigines' Rights Protection Society to stop the colonial government taking their lands; in 1920, Casely Hayford's National Congress of British West Africa carried the fight across the region; and in 1929 a young doctor of philosophy named Danquah helped found the Gold Coast Youth Conference. By 1948, soldiers who had fought for the Crown in Burma were home without their pay, prices were climbing, and a Ga chief, Nii Kwabena Bonne the Third, was leading a boycott of the European trading firms. The ground was hot before the two men ever met.

### 3. QUESTION TWO · 1:40
**Pinned card: "Who brought Nkrumah home?"**

> **10:33 PM · Baaba:** Honest question. Who actually brought Nkrumah back to the Gold Coast?
> **10:34 PM · Kwame:** here we go 🍿

*[B-roll: Saltpond; Paa Grant (archival, credited); Lincoln University, Pennsylvania; a 1947 ocean liner. Dateline: "SALTPOND · 4 AUGUST 1947".]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** Baaba, The Ascendant, asked the question one side of this argument always asks: who brought Nkrumah home? On the fourth of August 1947, at Saltpond, the United Gold Coast Convention was born, funded by the merchant George Alfred Grant, whom everybody called Paa Grant, with Danquah as its leading thinker. They needed a full-time secretary; the lawyer Ako Adjei, who had studied with Nkrumah at Lincoln University in America, turned the job down and recommended his friend, Paa Grant paid the fare, and on the twenty-eighth of December 1947 Nkrumah was presented to the Working Committee. Within months he had planted branches across the colony.

*[Dateline: "CHRISTIANSBORG CROSSROADS · SATURDAY, 28 FEBRUARY 1948". B-roll: the Osu crossroads today; the ex-servicemen memorial; 1948 riot footage; a newspaper front page; a map of the Northern Territories.]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** Then came Saturday, the twenty-eighth of February 1948. Unarmed ex-servicemen marching to the castle were stopped at the Christiansborg crossroads, and Superintendent Colin Imray opened fire, killing Sergeant Adjetey, Corporal Attipoe and Private Odartey Lamptey; Accra burned for days. On the twelfth of March the colonial government arrested six Convention leaders, Nkrumah, Danquah, Akufo-Addo, Ako Adjei, Obetsebi-Lamptey and William Ofori-Atta, and sent them north. Here is the one per cent: "Big Six" was never a national title. According to the historian Yaw Anokye Frimpong, it began as a newspaper caption the morning after, and other leaders, Paa Grant among them, were simply not caught in the swoop.

### 4. QUESTION THREE · 3:05
**Pinned card: "Now, or in the shortest possible time?"**

> **10:55 PM · Kwame:** ok but if Danquah's people brought him, why did Nkrumah leave them?? betrayal or what 🤷🏾‍♂️

*[B-roll: the Accra Evening News masthead; the Coussey Committee; a mass rally. Typewriter card: "SELF-GOVERNMENT NOW" vs "IN THE SHORTEST POSSIBLE TIME". Dateline: "ACCRA · 12 JUNE 1949".]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** Kwame was not having it: if Danquah's people brought him, why did Nkrumah leave them? Because the two men wanted the same thing at different speeds. The Convention asked for self-government "in the shortest possible time"; the young crowds following Nkrumah wanted it now. After the detentions the leadership moved him from secretary to treasurer, and he started his own paper, the Accra Evening News, on the third of September 1948. When the governor set up a committee under Justice Henley Coussey to write a new constitution, Danquah's colleagues sat on it and Nkrumah was left out. On the twelfth of June 1949, before a crowd put at about sixty thousand in Accra, he launched the Convention People's Party under three words: Self-Government Now.

*[B-roll: James Fort prison; 1951 ballot boxes; the release footage (12 Feb 1951, credited); Independence night, 6 March 1957; Old Polo Grounds. Dateline: "JAMES FORT · MONDAY, 12 FEBRUARY 1951 · ABOUT MIDDAY".]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** In January 1950 he called Positive Action, strikes and boycotts, was arrested before the month was out, and was sentenced to three years. From a cell in James Fort he stood in the election of the eighth of February 1951; his party won thirty-four of the thirty-eight elected seats, the old Convention won three, one of them Danquah's, and at about midday on the twelfth of February Nkrumah walked out of prison to lead the government. He became Prime Minister in 1952 and won again in 1954 and 1956, while Danquah lost his seat both times; on the sixth of March 1957 it was Nkrumah who declared Ghana free. Even the name carries both men: Danquah was arguing for "New Ghana" in print as early as 1934, critics credit an older researcher, the Reverend J. B. Anaman, and it was Nkrumah's government that put the name on the map.

### 5. QUESTION FOUR · 4:40
**Pinned card: "How did one die in the other's prison?"**

> **11:31 PM · Aba:** Nobody taught us this part. How did Danquah end up DYING in prison under Nkrumah?? 😶
> *(typing…)*

*[B-roll: 1960 election posters; the Preventive Detention Act gazette; Nsawam Prison exterior. Dateline: "NSAWAM · THURSDAY, 4 FEBRUARY 1965".]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** Aba, The Restless, typed what most textbooks skip: then how did one of them die in the other one's prison? In April 1960 Danquah ran against Nkrumah for president and took about eleven per cent to Nkrumah's eighty-nine. Under the Preventive Detention Act of 1958 he was held without trial from October 1961 to June 1962, then arrested again on the eighth of January 1964, six days after a police constable fired at the President and killed his bodyguard. On the fourth of February 1965, aged sixty-nine, Danquah died of a heart attack in the condemned block at Nsawam Prison, detained without ever facing a trial.

*[Dateline: "ACCRA · THURSDAY, 24 FEBRUARY 1966". B-roll: coup-day footage; the beheaded Parliament House statue (archival, credited); burning books; the NLC booklet cover "The Rebirth of Ghana: The End of Tyranny" (1966); KNUST gate; the Nkrumah Memorial Park.]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** A year and twenty days later, on the twenty-fourth of February 1966, soldiers and police overthrew Nkrumah while he was abroad, and the eraser turned the other way. His statue at Parliament House was pulled down and broken up, his portraits were torn from the walls, his books went onto bonfires, and his party was banned; the university at Kumasi lost his name, and the Ministry of Information printed a booklet called The Rebirth of Ghana: The End of Tyranny. The same new rulers gave Danquah a national funeral. Nkrumah died in exile in 1972, and his name went back onto the Kumasi university only in 1998. From those years Ghana's memory split into two camps: the Danquah-Busia-Dombo tradition that became today's NPP, and the Nkrumah legacy, carried by the CPP and championed in government today by the NDC.

## PRODUCTION NOTES

**Even-handed checks built in:**
- Nkrumah: the mass movement, the 1951–56 election wins and independence are set beside the Preventive Detention Act and Danquah's death in detention.
- Danquah: the UGCC, the fare, the name campaign and the Youth Conference are set beside his electoral defeats in 1954 and 1956, and the critics who dispute his credit for the name.
- The NLC's erasure of Nkrumah is told as fact. So is the NLC's honouring of Danquah.
- Both party camps are named, and each is described the way it describes its own lineage.
- Hundeyin and Boakye-Danquah each get one line, quoted or paraphrased, with no verdict.

**Claims written with attribution on purpose:**
- "Big Six" as a newspaper caption: this is Yaw Anokye Frimpong's account (GhanaWeb, Aug 2024). Prof. Atuguba made a similar "there was no Big Six" argument the same week. The script attributes the claim and does not assert it. The same historian also says most of the six were "CPP members". That is wrong (the CPP did not exist until 1949), so the script leaves it out.
- The name "Ghana": Danquah's 1928 handbook and his 1934 "New Ghana" column are documented. Nii Moi Thompson (CitiNewsroom, Aug 2023) credits Rev. J. B. Anaman's 1895 research and says Nkrumah chose the name. Anokye Frimpong says Danquah's own proposal was "Akanman". The script credits critics with the Anaman point and says only that Danquah argued for "New Ghana", which the record shows.
- Danquah as "traitor" or "CIA" figure: left out of the spoken script. These are allegations (raised in Parliament in Feb 2025 and contested), and they can't be resolved in eight minutes. They are recorded in the source notes.
- The CIA role in the 1966 coup: left out on purpose (it was covered in Special No. 1 and isn't needed here).
- Nkrumah's January 1950 arrest is dated 21 or 22 January in different sources, so the script says "before the month was out".
- Salary figures for Nkrumah's UGCC job differ between sources (£100 a month vs £250), so the script leaves them out.

**"The document" on wiping out Nkrumah's memory:** the script names the NLC/Ministry of Information booklet *The Rebirth of Ghana: The End of Tyranny* (Accra, 1966, 56 pages) as the official text of the erasure. If Joshua meant a different document (a decree, a CIA memo, or another file), send it and the line can be swapped.
