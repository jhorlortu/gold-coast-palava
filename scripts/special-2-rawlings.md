### 2. QUESTION ONE · 0:50
**Pinned card: "What exactly did he inherit?"**

> **11:19 PM · Aba:** Before we judge, what did he actually take over? I only know the June 4 songs 😅

*[B-roll: Burma Camp gates; archival 1979 newspapers; dateline: "ACCRA · 15 MAY 1979".]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** Aba, The Restless, asked the fair question first: what exactly did he take over? On the fifteenth of May 1979, a young flight lieutenant and a handful of soldiers tried to overthrow General Akuffo's government and failed; he was tried, sentenced to death, and locked up. On the fourth of June, soldiers broke him out, and within hours he was chairman of the Armed Forces Revolutionary Council, which ruled for one hundred and twelve days, executed eight senior officers, among them three former heads of state, Acheampong, Akuffo and Afrifa, and then, on the twenty-fourth of September, handed power to the elected President Hilla Limann. On the last day of 1981 he came back, and this time he stayed.

*[B-roll: dry farmland; smoke over bush; the red-white-and-blue checked "Ghana Must Go" bag. Typewriter caption: "THE 1%".]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** Now the one per cent most of us forget: 1983 hit Ghana like three storms at once. Inflation reached one hundred and twenty-three per cent, the worst drought in decades dried the farms, bushfires burned what was left, and in January Nigeria ordered illegal migrants out, so more than a million Ghanaians came home carrying the checked bags we still call Ghana Must Go. So before anybody talks transformation, understand this: his first job was not building a new Ghana; it was stopping the old one from collapsing.

> **11:31 PM · Aba:** Ei. So the house was on fire before the man entered 😳

### 3. QUESTION TWO · 2:15
**Pinned card: "People's power, or one man's power?"**

> **11:38 PM · Kwame:** Rescue, fine. But "power to the people"? Abeg. Soldiers, curfews, tribunals 🤨

*[B-roll: archival PDC rallies; Makola Market; a tribunal courtroom; candle and three empty judges' chairs.]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** Kwame was not done: power to the people, abeg, with soldiers and curfews? The revolution promised exactly that. It set up People's and Workers' Defence Committees in neighbourhoods and workplaces, later renamed Committees for the Defence of the Revolution, so ordinary Ghanaians could watch prices, expose corruption and hold power to account. But beside that people's power sat a hard state: public tribunals, Makola Market demolished in the price-control war of 1982, and that same year three High Court judges and a retired army major were abducted and murdered, a crime that still haunts that era's memory. Even so, the same regime set up a National Commission on Democracy to ask Ghanaians what kind of government they wanted, and that slow conversation, through the eighties, is the road that eventually led to the 1992 Constitution.

**KOFI (V.O.):** The British scholar Jeffrey Haynes, who interviewed the key players over thirty years, puts it plainly: Rawlings wanted a revolution of popular democracy but never clearly defined the new system, and lacked the ideological and institutional capacity to build it. He was brilliant at pulling an old order down, and far less sure what to put in its place. Yet one piece did survive: in 1988 his government created a hundred and ten District Assemblies, two-thirds of their members elected, and the assembly in your own district today is a grandchild of that law.

> **11:52 PM · Baaba:** So the committees died but the district assemblies stayed. Interesting.
> **11:53 PM · Kwame:** hmm 🤔

### 4. QUESTION THREE · 4:00
**Pinned card: "Did the revolution sell out to the IMF?"**

> **12:04 AM · Baaba:** Wait. The anti-imperialist revolutionary went to the IMF and World Bank?? How?

*[B-roll: 1980s Accra queues; then filled market stalls; IMF/World Bank headquarters exteriors; a cedi note being devalued on screen.]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** Baaba, The Ascendant, spotted the contradiction that every serious study of this man wrestles with: how does a revolutionary who came to fight the old order end up with the IMF and the World Bank? In April 1983, with the country starving, the government launched the Economic Recovery Programme: deep devaluation, price controls lifted, state firms trimmed, and foreign money flowing in. By the World Bank's figures, inflation fell from one hundred and twenty-three per cent in 1983 to forty per cent in 1986 and eighteen per cent by 1991, and the economy grew by about five per cent a year, until Ghana became the West's favourite example of adjustment that worked.

**KOFI (V.O.):** And remember how far we had fallen: at independence in 1957, Ghana's income per head stood at about the level of South Korea's, and by 1983 we were queueing for basic essentials. Was that a rescue or a retreat? Both. The shelves filled, but urban workers swallowed the pain, and the dream of a self-reliant people's economy quietly gave way to a market economy run by a soldier. Kevin Shillington, writing in 1992 as Ghana prepared for the Fourth Republic, asked how real that recovery felt to the ordinary Ghanaian; three decades on, the question still stands.

> **12:21 AM · Baaba:** So he saved the economy by abandoning his own ideology 😶

### 5. QUESTION FOUR · 5:20
**Pinned card: "Then why didn't nineteen years transform us?"**

> **12:44 AM · Amo:** Aba read me everything. Let me ask the real one. Why didn't 19 years change us for good? 🧐

*[B-roll: the cover of "Working with Rawlings" (Digibooks, 2020), credited; a toy aeroplane on a cabinet table; then the 1992 ballot box; the Kume Preko march, 11 May 1995; Jubilee House gates.]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** Then Amo, The Discerning, who had Aba reading the whole chat to him, asked the question this Special exists for. One witness is Kwamena Ahwoi, who worked beside Rawlings from the PNDC into the NDC years and published Working with Rawlings in 2020. He calls him a born leader with a mesmerising charisma, but writes that Rawlings had no plan and no strategy on how to materially develop Ghana, and that in meetings he sometimes looked out of the window or played with his toy planes while technical matters were debated. Rawlings' office replied that the book was generously economical with the truth, and rejected many of its claims, so take it as one insider's account, not the final word.

**KOFI (V.O.):** There is also the trap of success. The outsider became the establishment: he won the 1992 election with about sixty per cent, and when the opposition boycotted the parliamentary vote his party took almost every seat; in 1995, when crowds marched against the new VAT under the cry of Kume Preko, four people were shot dead by unidentified gunmen. He won again in 1996, with fifty-seven per cent against Kufuor's forty, and then, crucially, he obeyed the two-term limit. Here is the irony: on the seventh of January 2001 he handed power to John Kufuor, the first peaceful transfer from a sitting government to the opposition in our history, and Kufuor had once served about eight months as a secretary in Rawlings' own first PNDC government, a move Ahwoi says was partly about keeping Nigeria's President Shagari, a friend of Kufuor's, on side for oil.

> **1:12 AM · Amo:** So: no blueprint, a broken economy, and then power got comfortable. That's three reasons, not one.
> **1:13 AM · Kwame:** ok fine. it's complicated 😤

> **1:31 AM · Aba:** Editor, verdict abeg. Then I'll sleep 😴

*[B-roll: a nightjar at dusk; a ledger with two columns being filled in typewriter type; young Ghanaians at a voting queue.]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** Close to half past one, Aba asked for a verdict, and Ahwoi himself gave me the best one, an old Akan proverb about the nightjar bird: Santrofi anomaa, wokye no a, wakye mmusuo; wogyae no a, wagyae adeɛ. Catch it and you have caught a curse; let it go and you have let go of something precious. That is the man in one line: the revolutionary who did not finish his revolution, the pragmatist who pulled Ghana back from collapse, the strongman who still gave us the constitution, the term limits and the handover that made our democracy. So here is the lesson, squad: anger can diagnose a nation, but only a plan can heal it, and nineteen years of power cannot replace one good blueprint. ## PRODUCTION NOTES

**Even-handed checks built in:** achievements (stabilisation, ERP recovery, District Assemblies, 1992 Constitution, two-term exit and the 2001 handover) sit beside the failings (AFRC executions, the 1982 abductions, Makola, Kume Preko deaths, an undefined revolutionary system). Ahwoi's claims are attributed and set against the Rawlings office's rebuttal. No verdict on who ordered the 1982 killings or the Kume Preko shootings.

**Source discipline:** Shillington is cited only for his 1992 question about how real the recovery was for ordinary Ghanaians; nothing after 1992 is credited to him. Post-1992 material comes from Ahwoi (2020), Haynes (2023), Wikipedia and news reports.

**Points to double-check before recording:**
- "Three High Court judges": Wikipedia lists Justices Koranteng-Addow, Sarkodie and Agyepong, plus Majors Sam Acquah (retired) and Dasana Nantogmah. The script says "a retired army major" for length; consider "two army officers" if you want both named.
- Kume Preko deaths: news reports say four; the shooters are described as unidentified. Kept as "unidentified gunmen".
- The 1992 parliamentary boycott: the main opposition parties boycotted the December 1992 parliamentary elections after disputing the presidential result; the NDC won 189 of 200 seats.
- Kufuor's eight months: from the Graphic review of Ahwoi's book; the Nigeria oil link is Ahwoi's account, reported via Graphic and the Kwamena Ahwoi Wikipedia entry.
- "Toy planes": Ahwoi, p. 232, as reported by Daily Guide and GhanaWeb (August 2020).
- The proverb: spelled as GhanaWeb printed it ("Santrofi amoma…"); I have used the standard Twi "anomaa". Please confirm the Twi and the phonetic respelling in the voiceover.

**Images for social assets:** greyscale, real images only. Book covers and archival newspaper pages are safer than photographs of Rawlings unless you hold rights.
