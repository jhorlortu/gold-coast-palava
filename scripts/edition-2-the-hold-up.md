### 3. LEAD STORY · 1:41
**"Brown gold"**

**KOFI:** *[TIMELINE graphic builds along the bottom of the screen as each date is spoken.]* The man from Teshie was Tetteh Quarshie, who sailed in 1870 to Fernando Po, the island we now call Bioko, came home about six years later with cocoa beans, and in 1879 planted them at Mampong in Akuapem; *[TIMELINE: 1879 Mampong]* the Basel missionaries insist they got there first, but from farms like his the crop spread across the forest belt until, by 1911, the Gold Coast was the largest cocoa producer in the world. *[TIMELINE: 1911 world No. 1]* In the first edition we learned that this land was named for its gold, *[Split image: gold nugget / cocoa pod. Caption: "The second gold"]* and here it found a second gold, a brown one, grown not on European plantations but on small family farms whose owners sold to whoever would buy.

The trouble was that only a handful would buy. In 1937 the two giants of the trade, Cadbury and the United Africa Company, drew up a buying agreement that thirteen firms, handling about ninety-four percent of the colony's cocoa, eventually signed, so the price a farmer got would no longer be bargained over but simply announced. *[GRAPHIC: 13 firms · 94% of the crop]* When the deal became public in October, with cocoa worth more than sixty percent of the colony's exports, the farmers did what nobody in London expected: they held up the crop, refusing to sell, and boycotted European shop goods except the things no household can do without, sugar, kerosene, matches and tobacco. *[TIMELINE: 1937 the hold-up]* *[SFX: a shop shutter coming down]* London sent a commission of inquiry, and the buying agreement was suspended, so years before the crossroads of 1948, it was cocoa farmers who first proved that this colony could organise and win. *[STAMP: "HELD UP"]*

What came next looked like protection: during the war Britain began buying the crop itself, and in 1947 the Cocoa Marketing Board was born as the single buyer and seller of every bean, meant to shield farmers from cartels and wild prices, and also, as it turned out, a very convenient purse for whoever governed. *[TIMELINE: 1947 Cocoa Marketing Board]* Hold that thought. *[A small gold pin drops onto the 1947 marker and stays there until Then vs Now.]* When the board paid too little in the decades after independence, farmers carried their beans to where they were paid better, west into Côte d'Ivoire, which overtook Ghana as the world's top producer in 1978; *[TIMELINE: 1978 Côte d'Ivoire overtakes]* then the drought and bushfires of 1983, reform, and a long climb back to about a million tonnes by the early 2020s. *[TIMELINE: 1983 low · 2020s comeback]*

### 4. THE WIRE · 3:39
**"Thursday, 12 February 2026, Accra"**

*[Dateline card in Special Elite. Harmattan haze grade. Ticker underneath.]*

**KOFI:** On Thursday the twelfth of February 2026, in the dry, hazy weeks of the harmattan, the Minister for Finance, Dr Cassiel Ato Forson, announced in Accra something no Ghanaian government had done in living memory: he cut the price paid to cocoa farmers in the middle of the season, from fifty-eight thousand cedis a tonne to forty-one thousand, three hundred and ninety-two, so a sixty-four kilo bag that had been worth about three thousand six hundred cedis to a farmer in Sefwi was suddenly worth, how machay? Two thousand five hundred and eighty-seven. *[PRICE CARD: GH¢58,000 → GH¢41,392 per tonne · GH¢2,587 per 64kg bag]* The reason was simple: the world price, which had shot past ten thousand dollars a tonne at the end of 2024, had fallen below four thousand one hundred, while Ghana's own price, raised in October to stop beans being smuggled west, now stood above what the world would pay, so the buyers stayed away, the bags sat in sheds, and many farmers had waited months for their money. *[CHART: world cocoa price, above US$10,000 (late 2024) to below US$4,100 (Feb 2026)]*

Underneath it lay a broken habit. For thirty-two years a syndicated loan from foreign banks paid for each harvest, secured by selling most of the crop forward, as raw beans, before they were even picked; *[GRAPHIC: a bag of beans marked "SOLD" before the pod is open]* during the debt crisis, under the previous administration, that loan failed, and foreign traders were asked to pre-finance the crop instead. In Western North farmers marched against the cut, and Parliament has since passed a new COCOBOD law that guarantees farmers at least seventy percent of the export price, swaps the foreign loans for cedi bonds, and reserves at least half of every crop to be processed here at home. *[CARD: COCOBOD Bill 2026 · 70% of FOB guaranteed · cedi bonds · 50% processed at home]*

### 5. THE PALAVA DESK · 5:04
**"Sacks out, wrappers back"**

**KOFI:** Which brings us back to the question on that chocolate wrapper, and there are two truths here, and neither leaves the table. The first is that the money in cocoa has never lived on the farm: a study cited by the United Nations food agency found that the brands and shops at the end of the chain take about seventy percent of a chocolate bar's value, while the farmer takes about eleven. *[GRAPHIC: a chocolate bar split into shares, 70% brands and retailers, 11% farmer.]* We built a whole system to sell beans, so the loan that paid our farmers was secured with raw beans, and our grinding factories, built to handle more than half a million tonnes a year, have run at under half that, surviving on the smaller, discounted light-crop beans and paying heavily for power and borrowing, while the state's own Cocoa Processing Company in Tema has reported losses for five years running. *[GAUGE: grinding capacity 500,000+ tonnes, under half used]* That is why this Editor says the export of raw beans must stop, because every stage we add at home, grinding, pressing, packing and branding, is a job here and dollars here, and a country selling butter, powder and chocolate is not ruined by one bad year in one raw price. *[STAMP: "EDITOR'S VIEW"]*

*[The table turns; the other side of the card is shown.]* The second truth, which the processors themselves put on the table, is that a mandate is not a market: a factory forced to buy beans at a high local price and sell its butter into a cheap world market bleeds, and processing ordered without finance and buyers can leave stock rotting in warehouses at the people's cost. The budget talks of ending raw exports by 2030, so the real question is whether the factories, the power and the buyers arrive before that deadline or after the warehouses fill.

### 6. THEN VS NOW · 6:34

**KOFI:** Then, in 1947, a colony built a single state buyer for its cocoa; now a republic has built one for its gold. *[Split screen: 1947 Cocoa Marketing Board / 2025 GoldBod. The gold pin from the Lead Story timeline returns.]* The Ghana Gold Board, GoldBod, set up in 2025, is the only body allowed to buy, assay and export the gold of small-scale miners, about a hundred and four tonnes of it in 2025, with up to fifty-four more in the first half of this year, and its chief executive says eighty to ninety percent of what it exports is now refined in Ghana, *[CARD: GoldBod 2025: about 104 tonnes bought · 80 to 90% refined in Ghana (CEO)]* so the gold board is doing in a year what the cocoa board has struggled with for nearly eighty, though what those first accounts cost the Bank of Ghana is still argued over, and that belongs to our fifth edition. *[ON SCREEN: "More in Edition 5"]* Cocoa's story is also gold's warning: a single buyer that pays too little watches its product walk across the border, and a board that borrows against its own crop loses control of it. *[CARD: "What cocoa teaches gold"]*

And here is the smallest item on page one, the one in the corner: the smuggling has turned around. For decades our beans went west, but this year, with Ghana paying more than its neighbour, officials accused purchasing clerks of buying cheaper Ivorian cocoa, at about twelve hundred cedis a bag, and passing it off as Ghanaian. *[The corner classified ad from page one enlarges to full screen: THE 1%. Map arrow across the western border flips direction.]* E no be small thing. And some of that gold is now being dug out of cocoa farms, a story we are saving for this season's last edition. *[ON SCREEN: "Edition 10"]*

**KOFI:** Back at the Nest, the handwriting on that sticky note belongs to Baaba, whom the Nest has decided to call The Ascendant, the quietest person in the room and very often the cleverest, who grew up in a home where school fees came from a family cocoa farm and every report card was read aloud like a royal letter. *[Baaba's desk: neat stacks, a calculator, a school photo propped against her monitor.]* This February, for the first time she can remember, the cocoa money did not come, and instead of complaining she did what Baaba does: she bought a bar of imported chocolate, wrote eleven percent across the wrapper in red ink, the farmer's share, and left it on the shared table. *[Close-up: the wrapper, "11%" written in red ink.]* When Kwame reached for a square she slid the wrapper toward him, and he put his hand down; the thing pain me oo, his face said, and for once The Combative had no reply.
