## 1. Cold open · 0:00

At nineteen minutes to eleven on Tuesday night, the twenty-fourth of November, almost forty-one years to the week since it happened, Aba, The Restless, dropped a photo of a book page into the team's WhatsApp group. The book is Kwamena AH-woy's memoir, Working with ROLL-ings, and the page described a list of nearly one hundred Ghanaians that the CIA claimed as its own, with their spouses, children and parents, down to home addresses and the children's schools, all to be flown out of tah-MAH-lay airport on one American military transport plane, a C-one-thirty: about four hundred Ghanaians in a single flight, all because Ghana was holding one man the Americans wanted back.

In 1985, Ronald Reagan sat in the White House, and Jerry John ROLL-ings ruled Ghana as Chairman of the PNDC. Under their watch came what AH-woy calls one of the most audacious and dramatic incidents of international espionage: the exchange of America's Ghanaian "assets", Washington's polite word for spies, for one Ghanaian citizen.

Chale, how the thing dey go? I am Kofi, the Editor, and this is a Gold Coast Palava Special: no front page, no sections, just one love affair, two governments, a spy swap this country has half forgotten, and a group chat that went on past two in the morning. I take no side tonight: Washington called it espionage, Accra called it national security, and you can decide.

## 2. Who was Michael? · 0:55

Baaba, The Ascendant, wanted the man before the plane. Michael ag-BOH-twee soo-SOO-dis was born in Accra in April 1946, to a Greek father and a mother who was one of the ag-BOH-twee sisters, the same family as Jerry ROLL-ings' mother, which made him the Chairman's first cousin, although the American newspapers kept calling him a nephew. He grew up in West Germany, went to college in New York, and kept American permanent residence. When his cousin shot his way back into power on the last day of 1981, Michael set off for home but stopped in London, sending emissaries and still more emissaries ahead of him because, as the exile magazine Talking Drums put it, he was not sure what reception his mercurial cousin would give him. By 1983 he was a fixture of Accra's party circuit, debonair, fluent in German, and working for the intelligence machine of Captain KOH-joh tsee-KAH-tah.

## 3. How did the CIA's secrets walk out? · 1:45

Kwame, The Combative, refused to believe a Ghanaian could get inside the CIA. In May 1983, a twenty-seven-year-old clerk from rural Virginia, Sharon SKRAN-ij, arrived at the CIA station in the American embassy in Accra with top-secret clearance, not enthusiastic, she had written, but ready to make the best of it. She met Michael, and they became lovers. That same year, a CIA security officer having dinner at her house noticed a photograph on her dressing table of a shirtless Michael with a blanket pulled up to his chest; she later said she had told her station chief about the relationship, and was told only to be careful.

According to FBI testimony, when Michael asked her for the names of CIA officers and of the Ghanaians reporting to them, and she said she could not get them, he answered: "Yes you can, you are a CIA agent." Over about two years, the American indictment says, she gave him the identities of eleven informants and five CIA employees, the station chief among them, with cable traffic and details of a dissident plot to overthrow the government. The CIA's own internal study later recorded that she was the first known CIA employee to spy for an African intelligence service.

## 4. How did it fall apart? · 3:00

Aba asked how it ended, and it ended with a machine. Back home in 1985, Sharon sat a routine CIA lie-detector test; her answers about Michael set off alarms, and after two days with the FBI she confessed. Then she picked up the phone and asked him to meet her at the Springfield Holiday Inn in northern Virginia. On Wednesday, the tenth of July 1985, Michael walked in and FBI agents arrested him; Sharon was arrested the next day. He had about two thousand seven hundred dollars in his pocket, and told the court he could not afford a lawyer. Officials told reporters they believed at least one exposed informant in Ghana had been killed, a claim never proven in public, and the CIA pulled its people out of Accra. In Accra, the names Sharon had passed on were no longer a secret, and the men behind them were arrested.

## 5. How do you trade a spy? · 3:50

Amo, The Discerning, asked the real question: how do you trade? AH-woy, who was in the room, says four men handled it for Ghana: Justice D. F. AN-nan of the PNDC, Captain tsee-KAH-tah, the Foreign Secretary, Doctor Obed ah-sah-MOH-ah, who faced the American ambassador, and AH-woy himself, whose office oversaw the tribunals and who dealt with the CIA's station officer. America opened with the list of a hundred names and the plane at tah-MAH-lay. Ghana's counterintelligence advised refusal on three grounds: the list looked built to unsettle Ghana's own intelligence; a spy's sins could not be visited on his wife, his children and his parents; and an American military plane on Ghanaian soil was a danger, because, in the words AH-woy records, what if five hundred US Marines popped out of it?

The final deal, signed by ah-sah-MOH-ah and the American ambassador, reads like a film script. The courts in both countries would sit on a Saturday and a Sunday. Every accused would be found guilty. Every accused would get exactly twenty years. Michael would be flown to London Heathrow, the Ghanaians would be driven by road to LOH-may airport in Togo, where an American plane would be waiting, and the two flights would take off at the same moment. Michael lost his American residence, the Ghanaians could not come home without Accra's written consent, and the whole agreement was to stay secret.

## 6. Who were the eight? · 5:05

Of all those names, only eight men agreed to go, which AH-woy reads as an admission that they had worked for the CIA: soldiers, police officers, an embassy clerk, a Foreign Ministry researcher, and the heads of two state corporations. Abel eh-DOO-say, the tourism chief and a friend of AH-woy's family, flatly denied being an asset, said he believed ROLL-ings did not like him, took a day to consult his lawyer, and then accepted, as the surest way, he said, of getting his freedom back. Major John Kwaku ah-WOO-ah-cheh, days from retirement, was, by AH-woy's account, delighted: "The Americans, they want me? When can I leave?" Years later he told the National Reconciliation Commission that he had been stripped of his citizenship and deported, and the Commission called that an illegal act. Two memories of the same man; hold both.

In late November 1985, a convoy of about twenty vehicles, led by the Inspector General of Police, carried the eight from CID headquarters to LOH-may. Photographs of them embracing American officials reached the Ghanaian press, and Michael came home to women in white, singing groups, and woo-loh-MAY priests pouring libation for his return. That same week Ghana's donors met in Paris, and Talking Drums noted that a government living on IMF money could not afford to shout at Washington.

## 7. How do we even know? · 6:20

Baaba asked how we know any of this. For almost two decades it stayed sealed. Then, as the Reconciliation Commission reopened the case in the early two thousands, an American embassy press attaché named David Queen said on radio that he knew of no such agreement, although he had not even been in Ghana in 1985. AH-woy challenged the embassy to correct him, the embassy said nothing, and he took that silence as release from the secrecy clause, which is why, he says, he could finally tell the story in his 2020 book. Not everyone accepts his telling: Martin AH-mee-doo accused him of breaching state secrets, and Jerry ROLL-ings' office called parts of the book fabrications and grapevine accounts.

So, who won? Ghana's intelligence service got inside the CIA through a clerk and a charming cousin, then bargained a demand for four hundred people down to eight men; by any Cold War measure, that is a professional result. But Sharon SKRAN-ij went to prison, eight men lost their country, their families were left to follow, and an American claim of a killing has never been answered in public. Michael soo-SOO-dis died in Johannesburg in 2024, aged seventy-eight, and his body was flown home to libation once more. The deal was kept by two governments, and it was broken by one denial.
