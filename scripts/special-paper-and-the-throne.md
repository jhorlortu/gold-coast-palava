# GOLD COAST PALAVA SPECIAL — FULL SCRIPT
## "The Paper and the Throne"
Ghana's constitutions, 1960–1992, and the coups between them (this Special includes the former Edition 6, "Cold Chop")

A Gold Coast Palava Special (not an edition): no price line, no Dispatch, no Colophon, no Nest scenes.
Narrator: Kofi (the only voice). The team speaks only through on-screen WhatsApp text.
Voiceover (TTS copy): claude/special-paper-and-the-throne-voiceover.md · Research: claude/special-paper-and-the-throne-source-notes.md · Brief: claude/special-constitutions-brief.md

**Runtime:** about 1,550 spoken words, about 8 minutes 30 seconds at Kofi's pace (about 182 words a minute), or about 8 minutes 45 seconds with the title-card pauses. That is slightly over the usual 7–8 minutes for a Special. The brief left the length open because this Special covers five constitutions plus Cold Chop. To bring it under 8 minutes, see "If it must be 8 minutes" in the production notes.

**Editorial stance:** Kofi takes no side. Nkrumah's stated reasons sit beside his critics' case. Every coup gets its plotters' own justification, and the record of what followed. The democracy question ends with the evidence for and against and no verdict. Contested claims are credited to whoever makes them.

**The 1% in this Special:**
- The white stripe in Ghana's flag (1964–66)
- An English draftsman wrote the 1960 constitution, and it names Nkrumah inside the text
- The 1964 Act lets the President sack judges "for reasons which to him appear sufficient"
- Afrifa's court-martial was due the day after the coup
- The soldiers wrote their own exit into the 1969 constitution
- The 568 workers and "no court"
- The Electoral Commissioner who went over a wall in 1978
- Rawlings signed the Third Republic into force
- The 1979 constitution gave citizens a right to resist coups and fell anyway
- The 1992 referendum's 70%-and-35% rule
- The Fourth Republic has outlived all four earlier constitutions together, about two and a half times over
- Today's District Assemblies are a PNDC design

### 2. QUESTION ONE · 1:05
**Pinned card: "Why did Nkrumah do it?"**

> **9:58 PM · Baaba:** Honest question. Why would the man who freed us turn it into a one-party state?
> **9:59 PM · Kwame:** power. next question 🙄
> **9:59 PM · Baaba:** that's too easy, Kwame.

*[The 1960 gazette fills the screen. Highlighter sweeps across the text as Kofi names each article: Art 10 "KWAME NKRUMAH is hereby appointed first President"; Art 8(4) "shall act in his own discretion"; Art 55 "Special powers for first President". Dateline: "ACCRA · 1 JULY 1960".]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** Baaba, The Ascendant, refused the easy answer, so start with the paper. The Republic's first constitution, in force on the first of July 1960, was drafted largely by an Englishman, Francis Bennion, on loan from the Westminster drafting office. Article Ten writes a man's name into the law, Kwame Nkrumah, appointed first President. Article Eight says he acts in his own discretion and need not follow anyone's advice, and Article Fifty-Five lets him change almost any law by instrument, for as long as he stays in office.

*[B-roll: Kulungugu today, the small statue at the bomb site, a dusty road at the Burkina Faso border, schoolchildren in brown and yellow uniforms. Dateline: "KULUNGUGU · WEDNESDAY, 1 AUGUST 1962". Then: 1963 newspaper headline cards, credited: "Ghana Negates Justice" (Press, 13 Dec 1963).]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** Now his side of it. On the first of August 1962, returning from talks with Upper Volta's President Yaméogo at Tenkodogo, his convoy stopped at Kulungugu to greet schoolchildren, and a bomb went off; he survived, and people around him did not. In January 1964 a police constable fired on him at Flagstaff House. Nkrumah and his defenders saw a young state under siege: a violent federalist fight in the fifties, plots at home, Cold War hands abroad, and a Pan-African project he thought a divided parliament would strangle. His critics answer with a date. Preventive detention became law in 1958, four years before Kulungugu, and in the preface to his 1957 autobiography Nkrumah himself wrote that independence might need "emergency measures of a totalitarian kind".

> **10:16 PM · Amo 🎙️:** so he warned us. in 1957. 😶

*[The 1964 amendment slides out of the 1960 gazette. Typewriter close-ups of Act 224: "one national party… the Convention People's Party"; "for reasons which to him appear sufficient"; and Article 7, "the middle stripe white". Datelines: "SPECIAL COURT · MONDAY, 9 DECEMBER 1963", then "REFERENDUM · 24, 28 & 31 JANUARY 1964".]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** On the ninth of December 1963, Chief Justice Sir Arku Korsah's special court acquitted three of Nkrumah's own former lieutenants, Tawia Adamafio, Ako Adjei and Hugh Cofie-Crabbe, of plotting Kulungugu. Within days Korsah was dismissed, and the three stayed in detention. Then came the referendum of January 1964, which officially passed by ninety-nine point nine one per cent, while Time magazine reported "no" boxes sealed or missing in hundreds of places. The amendment it produced, Act two-two-four, made the Convention People's Party the one national party, let the President remove any judge "for reasons which to him appear sufficient", and changed the flag's middle stripe to white.

> **10:38 PM · Kwame:** fine. so how do you overthrow a man who owns the courts AND the flag?
> **10:39 PM · Amo 🎙️:** you wait till he travels

*[B-roll: Accra airport, 1966 (archival, credited); army trucks on the Kumasi–Accra road at night (stock, credited); Flagstaff House gate; radio-studio microphone. Dateline: "ACCRA · THURSDAY, 24 FEBRUARY 1966 · BEFORE DAWN". Then: Beijing airport, cold wind, Zhou Enlai on the tarmac (archival, credited). Dateline: "PEKING · SAME AFTERNOON".]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** Amo, The Discerning, who dictates his messages, had it right. Nkrumah left Accra on the twenty-first of February 1966, heading for Hanoi by way of Peking. On the twenty-third, by his own later account, the Kumasi garrison, about six hundred men, was ordered south "on manoeuvres". Before dawn on Thursday the twenty-fourth, Colonel Emmanuel Kotoka, Major Akwasi Afrifa and the police chief, John Harlley, struck in what was codenamed Operation Cold Chop. Flagstaff House held out for hours, and on the radio Kotoka told the nation that the myth surrounding Nkrumah had been broken. That afternoon in Peking, Premier Zhou Enlai stood waiting on the tarmac for a president who no longer had a country to govern. Here is the one per cent: Major Afrifa was due to face a court-martial for insubordination the very next morning. On the twenty-eighth of February the gold stripe went back on the flag.

### 4. QUESTION THREE · 3:55
**Pinned card: "How do soldiers give power back?"**

> **11:09 PM · Aba:** ok but after a coup, who writes the rules?? the soldiers?
> *(typing…)*

*[The 1969 decree, N.L.C.D. 406, fills the screen. Highlights land as Kofi reads: "All Decrees… are hereby confirmed"; "Presidential Commission"; "Gold Coast… Ghana". Signature block: Afrifa, Harlley, Deku, Ocran, Yakubu, Yeboah. Dateline: "ACCRA · 30 SEPTEMBER 1969". The 1960 gazette slides off the stack and hits the floor.]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** Aba, The Restless, asked who writes the rules after a coup, and the answer is a decree. The National Liberation Council ruled by decree for three and a half years, then wrote its own exit into the next constitution: the 1969 constitution let the soldiers keep making decrees until the new Parliament sat. Their last big decree, signed on the thirtieth of September 1969, confirmed every decree they had ever made as permanent law, handed the presidency to a three-man commission of soldiers and police, and, quietly, replaced the words "Gold Coast" in the old statutes with "Ghana". The new constitution answered 1964 clause by clause: a Prime Minister and Cabinet instead of an all-powerful President, the army chief appointed only on the advice of a Council of State, and treason defined in the constitution itself, so no Parliament could stretch it.

*[B-roll: Black Star Square, 1 October 1969 (archival); Busia portrait (credited); Daily Graphic front page, Saturday 21 February 1970 (credited). Dateline: "ACCRA · 21 FEBRUARY 1970". The 1969 gazette slides off. Dateline: "LONDON · THURSDAY, 13 JANUARY 1972".]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** It lasted under two and a half years. Busia's Progress Party won a hundred and five of a hundred and forty seats, and on the twenty-first of February 1970 its government dismissed five hundred and sixty-eight public officers under the new transitional clauses. One of them, E. K. Sallah, a manager at the state trading corporation, went to court and won, and the Prime Minister answered with the line still quoted at him: no court could force a government to employ anybody. On the thirteenth of January 1972, while Busia was in London for medical treatment, Colonel Ignatius Kutu Acheampong took over, and Busia called it "an officers' amenities coup".

*[B-roll: Unigov posters, 1978 (archival, credited); a typewritten letter on a desk (reconstruction, labelled "RECONSTRUCTION"); a compound wall at night. Dateline: "ACCRA · THURSDAY, 30 MARCH 1978".]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** The next soldiers tried to skip the paper altogether. Acheampong's Union Government, Unigov, would have been a state with no parties at all. At the referendum of the thirtieth of March 1978, the Electoral Commissioner, Justice Isaac Kobina Abban, went into hiding rather than declare the regime's figures, and one account says he went over the commission's wall to do it. Unigov was declared the winner, and within four months Acheampong's own officers had removed him.

### 5. QUESTION FOUR · 5:45
**Pinned card: "Why did 1979 fail?"**

> **11:52 PM · Baaba:** 1979 had a constitution too. what happened to it?

*[The 1979 gazette: the promulgation decree's signature block, "FLT. LT. JERRY JOHN RAWLINGS, Chairman of the Armed Forces Revolutionary Council", then Article 1(3) highlighted. Dateline: "ACCRA · 18 SEPTEMBER 1979". The 1979 gazette slides off. Dateline: "31 DECEMBER 1981".]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** Baaba asked about 1979, and here is a twist few remember. That constitution was drafted under the military council Rawlings overthrew in June 1979, and on the eighteenth of September it was Rawlings, as chairman of the Armed Forces Revolutionary Council, who signed it into force. It banned the one-party state, limited presidents to two four-year terms, and in Article One gave every citizen the right to resist anyone who tried to abolish it. It lasted twenty-seven months. On the thirty-first of December 1981, Rawlings came back.

### 6. QUESTION FIVE · 6:15
**Pinned card: "How did we get to 1992?"**

*[B-roll: District Assembly polling, 1988 (archival, credited); Ghana Bar Association statement headline, Oct 1990 (credited); IMF and World Bank logos on a newsprint card; Consultative Assembly in session, 1991 (archival); referendum ballot, 28 April 1992. Dateline: "ACCRA · TUESDAY, 28 APRIL 1992".]*

> **12:09 AM · Amo 🎙️:** rawlings didn't like parties. so who pushed him?

**KOFI (V.O.):** Amo asked who pushed. The PNDC first built its own model, "participatory democracy": defence committees, then elected District Assemblies from December 1988, with one-third of members appointed. Then the pressure came from both sides: the Bar Association, the churches, the unions and the students at home, and from the donors whose money had carried the recovery since 1983. A Committee of Experts drafted proposals, a two-hundred-and-sixty-seat Consultative Assembly wrote the text, and the referendum on the twenty-eighth of April 1992 needed two things to pass: seventy per cent "yes" and a thirty-five per cent turnout. It got ninety-two point six per cent, on a turnout of about forty-five. Article Three now says that defending the constitution is every citizen's right and duty, while the transitional provisions protect the PNDC's own past acts from the courts.

*[Map animation, newsprint: Côte d'Ivoire, Togo, Burundi each light up with a typewriter tag: "33 YEARS → 1999 COUP → 2002 WAR"; "38 YEARS → 2005 · 400–500 DEAD (UN)"; "THIRD TERM 2015 → HUNDREDS KILLED". Tanzania lights up in gold: "STEPPED DOWN · 1985".]*

**KOFI (V.O.):** Kwame asked the forbidden question, so look next door. Houphouët-Boigny ruled Côte d'Ivoire for thirty-three years in calm; six years after he died came its first coup, and three years after that, civil war. Eyadéma ruled Togo for thirty-eight years; when he died in 2005 the army installed his son, and the United Nations counted four to five hundred dead. In Burundi in 2015, a third-term bid left hundreds dead and hundreds of thousands in exile. Tanzania's Nyerere also ran a one-party state, but he stepped down in 1985 and the country held. Across the record, the danger is usually not the long reign; it is the exit nobody planned. That is a pattern, not a prophecy.

## PRODUCTION NOTES

**Even-handed checks built in:**
- Nkrumah's own case is given: Kulungugu, the 1964 shooting, the 1950s violence, Cold War pressure and the Pan-African project. So is the critics' answer: the Preventive Detention Act came first, and his 1957 preface. Both are in his own words or the record's.
- The 1964 referendum figure is given as official, beside a contemporary press report of sealed "no" boxes. It is credited to Time and not asserted.
- The coup plotters' stated reason (Kotoka's broadcast) is given, and so is the motive question (Afrifa's court-martial). The script leaves the motive question to Baaba and Kwame and does not assert it.
- The NLC gets credit for building its own exit and the 1969 safeguards. Busia gets the Sallah dismissals and his "no court" remark, and also his own description of the coup against him.
- Rawlings appears both as the man who signed 1979 into force and as the man who ended it. The 1992 transition credits both domestic pressure and donor pressure.
- The counterfactual is given as a pattern from other countries with a stable counter-example (Tanzania), and Kofi says outright that it is "not a prophecy".
- The democracy question gives both sides from the record and ends without a verdict.

**Claims written with care on purpose:**
- **Flag dates:** sources give 1 January 1964 for the change (FOTW), while Act 224, which wrote "white" into Article 7, received assent on 21 February 1964. So the script says "for about two years" and does not give a start date. The restoration on 28 February 1966 is from Britannica.
- **Korsah's dismissal:** sources give 11 or 13 December 1963. The script says "within days".
- **1964 referendum:** the official figure (99.91%) is from the African Elections Database via Wikipedia. Time (14 Feb 1964) reported slightly different numbers and the sealed "no" boxes. The Act gives three polling days (24, 28 and 31 January), so the script says "the referendum of January 1964".
- **Kulungugu:** the script says "people around him did not" survive, because casualty counts vary. The bomb's method (thrown or planted) is disputed, so it isn't described.
- **The January 1964 shooting:** this was also covered in "The Men Who Founded Ghana", and it is kept to one clause here.
- **"About six hundred men… on manoeuvres":** this is Nkrumah's own account (Dark Days in Ghana, as excerpted by GhanaWeb), and the script says "by his own later account".
- **Afrifa's court-martial on 25 February:** from Wikipedia's 1966 coup article, which cites Baynham. It is written as a fact of the record. The question of motive is left to the chat.
- **CIA role in 1966:** left out on purpose (it was covered in Special No. 1).
- **Busia's "no court":** the exact wording varies between reports, so the script paraphrases it. "An officers' amenities coup" is Busia's own phrase, as quoted in Africa-history sources.
- **Abban going over the wall:** this is one account (ModernGhana, 2008). A University of Sussex library blog confirms that he went into hiding and wrote to Acheampong from hiding. The script attributes the wall to "one account".
- **Unigov official figures:** sources differ (60.1% on Wikipedia; other sources give other tallies), so no percentage is spoken.
- **Burundi casualties:** sources range from 400 to 1,200 dead and from 270,000 to 450,000 refugees, so the script says "hundreds" and "hundreds of thousands".
- **"Four times":** the turnovers in 2000, 2008, 2016 and 2024 are each a change of governing party.
- **The 1992 referendum rule** (70% yes, 35% turnout) and the result (92.59%, 3,680,974 votes cast of 8,255,690 registered, about 44.6%) are from Wikipedia citing the African Elections Database.
- **Lifespans:** 1957 (3.3 yrs) + 1960 (5.7) + 1969 (2.4) + 1979 (2.3) = 13.6 yrs. The 1992 constitution is 33.9 yrs as of 26 Nov 2026, about 2.5 times as long.
- **Ministers from Parliament:** Article 78 says the majority of ministers must come from Parliament.

**Callbacks, without repeating:** Danquah's death in detention and the burning of Nkrumah's books are left to "The Men Who Founded Ghana". The AFRC executions are left to Special No. 2. The 1982 judges are left to Edition 5. The CIA's role in 1966 is left to Special No. 1.
