## "The Men Who Founded Ghana: Nkrumah and Danquah"

Spoken words only, for recording · Narrator: Kofi · Target 7 to 8 minutes (about 1,360 words)

Kofi is pronounced KOH-fee (Akan tone: low on KOH, high on fee; never KAW-fee or koh-FEE). Companion file: claude/special-men-who-founded-ghana.md

## 1. Cold open · 0:00

At fourteen minutes past ten on Monday night, the sixteenth of November, four days after the Bank of Ghana unveiled its new Heritage Series notes, Kwame, The Combative, dropped a photo into the team's WhatsApp group: en-KROO-mah was on the new money, and the old portrait of the Big Six was gone. Under it he pasted a headline, the Minority Leader, ah-FEN-yoh MAR-kin, telling the government that you can remove the Big Six from the currency, but not from history; then he typed the question that has divided this country for seventy years: so who founded Ghana, one man or six?

My squad, how you dey? I am KOH-fee, the Editor, and this is a Gold Coast Palava Special: no front page, no sections, just two men, Kwame en-KROO-mah and Joseph BOH-ah-cheh DAHN-kwah, the facts as the archives keep them, and a group chat that refused to sleep. I am not picking a side tonight; I am putting the facts in order so that you can judge.

## 2. What was here before them? · 0:50

Amo, The Discerning, who dictates his messages, asked us to start where most of these arguments never start: before either man. The Gold Coast of the nineteen-forties was a British colony ruled from KRIS-tchanz-borg Castle, and resistance did not begin with a political party. On the fourth of August 1897, lawyers and chiefs on the coast formed the Aborigines' Rights Protection Society to stop the colonial government taking their lands; in 1920, KAY-sly HAY-ford's National Congress of British West Africa carried the fight across the region; and in 1929 a young doctor of philosophy named DAHN-kwah helped found the Gold Coast Youth Conference. By 1948, soldiers who had fought for the Crown in Burma were home without their pay, prices were climbing, and a Ga chief, NEE kwah-BEH-nah BON-neh the Third, was leading a boycott of the European trading firms. The ground was hot before the two men ever met.

## 3. Who brought Nkrumah home? · 1:40

Baaba, The Ascendant, asked the question one side of this argument always asks: who brought en-KROO-mah home? On the fourth of August 1947, at Saltpond, the United Gold Coast Convention was born, funded by the merchant George Alfred Grant, whom everybody called PAH Grant, with DAHN-kwah as its leading thinker. They needed a full-time secretary; the lawyer AH-koh AH-jay, who had studied with en-KROO-mah at Lincoln University in America, turned the job down and recommended his friend, PAH Grant paid the fare, and on the twenty-eighth of December 1947 en-KROO-mah was presented to the Working Committee. Within months he had planted branches across the colony.

Then came Saturday, the twenty-eighth of February 1948. Unarmed ex-servicemen marching to the castle were stopped at the Christiansborg crossroads, and Superintendent Colin IM-ray opened fire, killing Sergeant ah-jeh-TAY, Corporal AH-tee-poh-ay and Private oh-DAR-tay LAMP-tee; Accra burned for days. On the twelfth of March the colonial government arrested six Convention leaders, en-KROO-mah, DAHN-kwah, ah-KOO-foh AH-doh, AH-koh AH-jay, oh-bet-SEH-bee LAMP-tee and William oh-FOH-ree AH-tah, and sent them north. Here is the one per cent: "Big Six" was never a national title. According to the historian Yaw ah-NOH-cheh FRIM-pong, it began as a newspaper caption the morning after, and other leaders, PAH Grant among them, were simply not caught in the swoop.

## 4. Now, or in the shortest possible time? · 3:05

Kwame was not having it: if DAHN-kwah's people brought him, why did en-KROO-mah leave them? Because the two men wanted the same thing at different speeds. The Convention asked for self-government "in the shortest possible time"; the young crowds following en-KROO-mah wanted it now. After the detentions the leadership moved him from secretary to treasurer, and he started his own paper, the Accra Evening News, on the third of September 1948. When the governor set up a committee under Justice Henley KUH-see to write a new constitution, DAHN-kwah's colleagues sat on it and en-KROO-mah was left out. On the twelfth of June 1949, before a crowd put at about sixty thousand in Accra, he launched the Convention People's Party under three words: Self-Government Now.

In January 1950 he called Positive Action, strikes and boycotts, was arrested before the month was out, and was sentenced to three years. From a cell in James Fort he stood in the election of the eighth of February 1951; his party won thirty-four of the thirty-eight elected seats, the old Convention won three, one of them DAHN-kwah's, and at about midday on the twelfth of February en-KROO-mah walked out of prison to lead the government. He became Prime Minister in 1952 and won again in 1954 and 1956, while DAHN-kwah lost his seat both times; on the sixth of March 1957 it was en-KROO-mah who declared Ghana free. Even the name carries both men: DAHN-kwah was arguing for "New Ghana" in print as early as 1934, critics credit an older researcher, the Reverend J. B. ah-NAH-man, and it was en-KROO-mah's government that put the name on the map.

## 5. How did one die in the other's prison? · 4:40

Aba, The Restless, typed what most textbooks skip: then how did one of them die in the other one's prison? In April 1960 DAHN-kwah ran against en-KROO-mah for president and took about eleven per cent to en-KROO-mah's eighty-nine. Under the Preventive Detention Act of 1958 he was held without trial from October 1961 to June 1962, then arrested again on the eighth of January 1964, six days after a police constable fired at the President and killed his bodyguard. On the fourth of February 1965, aged sixty-nine, DAHN-kwah died of a heart attack in the condemned block at en-SAH-wahm Prison, detained without ever facing a trial.

A year and twenty days later, on the twenty-fourth of February 1966, soldiers and police overthrew en-KROO-mah while he was abroad, and the eraser turned the other way. His statue at Parliament House was pulled down and broken up, his portraits were torn from the walls, his books went onto bonfires, and his party was banned; the university at Kumasi lost his name, and the Ministry of Information printed a booklet called The Rebirth of Ghana: The End of Tyranny. The same new rulers gave DAHN-kwah a national funeral. en-KROO-mah died in exile in 1972, and his name went back onto the Kumasi university only in 1998. From those years Ghana's memory split into two camps: the DAHN-kwah, BOO-sya, DOM-boh tradition that became today's NPP, and the en-KROO-mah legacy, carried by the CPP and championed in government today by the NDC.

## 6. So who founded Ghana? · 6:00

Then Baaba found the detail that explains the noise on your timeline. For years the twenty-first of September, en-KROO-mah's birthday, was Founder's Day, apostrophe before the s: one founder. In 2019, under President ah-KOO-foh AH-doh, DAHN-kwah's great-nephew, the law moved Founders' Day, apostrophe after the s, to the fourth of August, the birthday of both the 1897 society and the 1947 Convention, and renamed the twenty-first Kwame en-KROO-mah Memorial Day. In June 2025, under President MAH-ha-mah, Parliament reversed it: the fourth of August was dropped and the twenty-first became Founder's Day again. Two governments, one apostrophe, back and forth.

The fight now travels far beyond our borders. On Founder's Day this year, Dr Palgrave BOH-ah-cheh DAHN-kwah posted that Ghana wasn't built by one man; the next day the Nigerian journalist David hun-DAY-in, who runs The Spearhead, replied that the Big Six "will never be a thing", and his outlet has since called it contrived.

## 7. The verdict the archives allow · 7:00

Close to one in the morning, Kwame, of all people, asked me for a verdict, and here is what the archives allow. DAHN-kwah and the Convention built the platform and paid the fare that brought en-KROO-mah home; en-KROO-mah turned a lawyers' convention into a mass movement, won the votes the British could not ignore, and declared the nation free. Then the free nation detained DAHN-kwah until he died, and the coup that followed tried to delete en-KROO-mah. If you want one founder, the ballots of 1951 to 1956 carry en-KROO-mah's name; if you ask who laid the foundation, DAHN-kwah's name is in the concrete, beside the veterans at the crossroads, the market women, the chiefs and the youth associations who never made it onto a banknote. Move the apostrophe as you like; the facts do not move.
