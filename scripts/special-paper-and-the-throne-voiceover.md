## 1. Cold open · 0:00

At thirteen minutes to ten on Thursday night, the twenty-sixth of November, Kwame, The Combative, dropped an old black-and-white photograph into the team's WhatsApp group: an Accra street in the mid-sixties, and on the pole, our flag, except the gold stripe in the middle was white. It was not a filter. For about two years, Ghana's flag carried white in the place of gold, the colours of one party, written into the constitution itself. And it came down again in February 1966, four days after soldiers took the country.

Look around us tonight. Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger, Guinea, and just last November, Guinea-Bissau: soldiers in power. In December, Benin's coup failed within hours. Ghana, which saw five successful coups between 1966 and 1981, has lived under one constitution since 1993. How did we get here?

My people, what dey go on? I am Kofi, the Editor, and this is a Gold Coast Palava Special: five constitutions, five coups that succeeded, one question that will not go away, and a group chat that ran till half past one. I take no side tonight. I put the papers on the table, and you judge.

## 2. Why did Nkrumah do it? · 1:05

Baaba, The Ascendant, refused the easy answer, so start with the paper. The Republic's first constitution, in force on the first of July 1960, was drafted largely by an Englishman, Francis Bennion, on loan from the Westminster drafting office. Article Ten writes a man's name into the law, Kwame n-KROO-mah, appointed first President. Article Eight says he acts in his own discretion and need not follow anyone's advice, and Article Fifty-Five lets him change almost any law by instrument, for as long as he stays in office.

Now his side of it. On the first of August 1962, returning from talks with Upper Volta's President yah-may-OH-goh at ten-koh-DOH-goh, his convoy stopped at koo-loong-GOO-goo to greet schoolchildren, and a bomb went off; he survived, and people around him did not. In January 1964 a police constable fired on him at FLAG-staff House. n-KROO-mah and his defenders saw a young state under siege: a violent federalist fight in the fifties, plots at home, Cold War hands abroad, and a Pan-African project he thought a divided parliament would strangle. His critics answer with a date. Preventive detention became law in 1958, four years before koo-loong-GOO-goo, and in the preface to his 1957 autobiography n-KROO-mah himself wrote that independence might need "emergency measures of a totalitarian kind".

On the ninth of December 1963, Chief Justice Sir Arku KOR-sah's special court acquitted three of n-KROO-mah's own former lieutenants, Tawia ah-dah-mah-FEE-oh, AH-koh AD-jay and Hugh KOH-fee KRAB, of plotting koo-loong-GOO-goo. Within days KOR-sah was dismissed, and the three stayed in detention. Then came the referendum of January 1964, which officially passed by ninety-nine point nine one per cent, while Time magazine reported "no" boxes sealed or missing in hundreds of places. The amendment it produced, Act two-two-four, made the Convention People's Party the one national party, let the President remove any judge "for reasons which to him appear sufficient", and changed the flag's middle stripe to white.

## 3. How did the soldiers do it? · 2:55

Amo, The Discerning, who dictates his messages, had it right. n-KROO-mah left Accra on the twenty-first of February 1966, heading for Hanoi by way of Peking. On the twenty-third, by his own later account, the Kumasi garrison, about six hundred men, was ordered south "on manoeuvres". Before dawn on Thursday the twenty-fourth, Colonel Emmanuel koh-TOH-kah, Major Akwasi ah-FREE-fah and the police chief, John HAR-lee, struck in what was codenamed Operation Cold Chop. FLAG-staff House held out for hours, and on the radio koh-TOH-kah told the nation that the myth surrounding n-KROO-mah had been broken. That afternoon in Peking, Premier JOH en-LYE stood waiting on the tarmac for a president who no longer had a country to govern. Here is the one per cent: Major ah-FREE-fah was due to face a court-martial for insubordination the very next morning. On the twenty-eighth of February the gold stripe went back on the flag.

Meanwhile, the city that received him was months from Mao's Cultural Revolution, and in Indonesia another founding president, soo-KAR-noh, was handing power to his generals.

## 4. How do soldiers give power back? · 3:55

Aba, The Restless, asked who writes the rules after a coup, and the answer is a decree. The National Liberation Council ruled by decree for three and a half years, then wrote its own exit into the next constitution: the 1969 constitution let the soldiers keep making decrees until the new Parliament sat. Their last big decree, signed on the thirtieth of September 1969, confirmed every decree they had ever made as permanent law, handed the presidency to a three-man commission of soldiers and police, and, quietly, replaced the words "Gold Coast" in the old statutes with "Ghana". The new constitution answered 1964 clause by clause: a Prime Minister and Cabinet instead of an all-powerful President, the army chief appointed only on the advice of a Council of State, and treason defined in the constitution itself, so no Parliament could stretch it.

It lasted under two and a half years. boo-SEE-ah's Progress Party won a hundred and five of a hundred and forty seats, and on the twenty-first of February 1970 its government dismissed five hundred and sixty-eight public officers under the new transitional clauses. One of them, E. K. SAL-lah, a manager at the state trading corporation, went to court and won, and the Prime Minister answered with the line still quoted at him: no court could force a government to employ anybody. On the thirteenth of January 1972, while boo-SEE-ah was in London for medical treatment, Colonel Ignatius Kutu ah-CHAM-pong took over, and boo-SEE-ah called it "an officers' amenities coup".

The next soldiers tried to skip the paper altogether. ah-CHAM-pong's Union Government, YOO-nee-gov, would have been a state with no parties at all. At the referendum of the thirtieth of March 1978, the Electoral Commissioner, Justice Isaac Kobina AH-bahn, went into hiding rather than declare the regime's figures, and one account says he went over the commission's wall to do it. YOO-nee-gov was declared the winner, and within four months ah-CHAM-pong's own officers had removed him.

## 5. Why did 1979 fail? · 5:45

Baaba asked about 1979, and here is a twist few remember. That constitution was drafted under the military council ROLL-ings overthrew in June 1979, and on the eighteenth of September it was ROLL-ings, as chairman of the Armed Forces Revolutionary Council, who signed it into force. It banned the one-party state, limited presidents to two four-year terms, and in Article One gave every citizen the right to resist anyone who tried to abolish it. It lasted twenty-seven months. On the thirty-first of December 1981, ROLL-ings came back.

## 6. How did we get to 1992? · 6:15

Amo asked who pushed. The PNDC first built its own model, "participatory democracy": defence committees, then elected District Assemblies from December 1988, with one-third of members appointed. Then the pressure came from both sides: the Bar Association, the churches, the unions and the students at home, and from the donors whose money had carried the recovery since 1983. A Committee of Experts drafted proposals, a two-hundred-and-sixty-seat Consultative Assembly wrote the text, and the referendum on the twenty-eighth of April 1992 needed two things to pass: seventy per cent "yes" and a thirty-five per cent turnout. It got ninety-two point six per cent, on a turnout of about forty-five. Article Three now says that defending the constitution is every citizen's right and duty, while the transitional provisions protect the PNDC's own past acts from the courts.

## 7. What if he never left? · 7:00

Kwame asked the forbidden question, so look next door. oof-WET bwahn-YEE ruled Côte d'Ivoire for thirty-three years in calm; six years after he died came its first coup, and three years after that, civil war. eh-yah-DAY-mah ruled Togo for thirty-eight years; when he died in 2005 the army installed his son, and the United Nations counted four to five hundred dead. In Burundi in 2015, a third-term bid left hundreds dead and hundreds of thousands in exile. Tanzania's nyeh-REH-reh also ran a one-party state, but he stepped down in 1985 and the country held. Across the record, the danger is usually not the long reign; it is the exit nobody planned. That is a pattern, not a prophecy.

## 8. Is democracy what Ghana needs? · 7:40

Aba saved the hardest one for last. The case for: power has changed hands at the ballot box four times, in 2000, 2008, 2016 and 2024, when the governing party's candidate conceded the morning after the vote, and the Fourth Republic has now outlived all four earlier constitutions put together, about two and a half times over. The case against, from its own critics: a winner-takes-all presidency, most ministers drawn from Parliament, which blurs the check, disputed elections settled in court, patronage, and a return to the IMF in 2023. And the PNDC's model is not all gone: today's District Assemblies, with their appointed third, are its design.

Here is what the record allows. The paper that put one man above it fell, and so did every throne the soldiers sat on. But two good papers, 1969 and 1979, fell too, because a constitution lasts only as long as the people with guns, and the people with votes, choose to keep it. The one that has lasted made the chair temporary, and so far, everyone has kept it. Whether it is good enough is your argument to have. Keep it in the open.
