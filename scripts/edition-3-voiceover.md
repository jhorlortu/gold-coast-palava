## 1. The Nest · 0:00

This evening the shared table at the Nest holds a map you cannot read with your eyes alone, because every border on it has been traced in string and glued down, red for Britain, blue for France, black for Germany; and in our open newsroom, the whole team has watched one pair of hands move across it all afternoon, stopping again and again at the same town in the north-east, where the card underneath has been pressed thin. That town is Baw-koo, and whose hands they are, I will tell you before we close.

My people, what deh go on? I am Koh-fee, the Editor, this is the Gold Coast Pah-lah-vah, and the price of this third edition, paid as always in data, is about fifty megabytes, a small price for a map Ghana has spent seventy years living with. If you missed the first two editions, go back for them first, because the king who signed Ah-sahn-teh into a colony signed the north into a protectorate on the very same day, and the cocoa farms of our second edition were worked, season after season, by hands from the very north this story is about.

## 2. Front Page · 1:01

Page one, and as the house rule goes, every headline is a question. Our lead: how did a king's capital end up inside a German colony while most of his kingdom stayed British, and what did a group of islands in the Pacific Ocean have to do with it? Which Gold Coast soldier fired the first shot of the First World War for the British Empire, and why was it fired in Togoland? Why did one territory vote in nineteen fifty-six to join a country that did not yet exist, while four in ten said no? Why has a town in the Upper East slept under curfew since twenty twenty-one? And tucked in the corner like a classified ad: which decision, taken by colonial officers almost a century ago, is still deciding who rules in Baw-koo tonight?

## 3. Lead Story · 1:44

Start in Berlin, where from November eighteen eighty-four to February eighteen eighty-five the powers of Europe agreed the rules for dividing Africa, with not one African in the room; Germany had already raised its flag east of the Volta that July and called it Togoland. Inland, Britain and Germany both wanted the trading country around Sah-lah-gah and Yen-dee, so they declared it a neutral zone, which held only until the Germans marched on Yen-dee, and on the third and fourth of December eighteen ninety-six the Dahg-bahm-bah fought them at Ah-dee-boh, the battle Dahg-bong still calls Ah-dee-boh dah-lee, the day of Ah-dee-boh, before the Germans burned Yen-dee to the ground.

Then, on the fourteenth of November eighteen ninety-nine, came a deal so strange it belongs on page one: in a treaty mostly about who would keep the islands of Samoa, on the other side of the world, Britain and Germany also split the neutral zone, so Yen-dee, the seat of the Yah Nah, went to Germany, while Sah-lah-gah and the western part of Dahg-bong stayed British. On the twenty-sixth of September nineteen oh one, King Edward the Seventh signed Orders in Council on one day that made Ah-sahn-teh a colony, the ending of our first edition, and created the Northern Territories of the Gold Coast, a protectorate in force from the first of January nineteen oh two.

The pencil moved again in nineteen fourteen. Days after Britain declared war on Germany, the Gold Coast Regiment marched into Togoland, and on the seventh of August nineteen fourteen, near Nwah-chah, a soldier named Al-hah-jee Groon-shee returned fire on a German-led police patrol, the first shot fired by a soldier in British service in the whole First World War, less than three weeks before the colony surrendered. Britain and France then cut Togoland in two, and the new line put Yen-dee back with Dahg-bong on the British side but sliced the Eh-weh people in half.

## 4. The Wire · 3:24

On Wednesday the ninth of May nineteen fifty-six, in the first weeks of the rainy season, when the farm roads around Hoh and Kpahn-doo turn to red mud, the people of British Togoland queued to decide their future under the eye of a United Nations commissioner, Eh-dwar-doh Es-pee-noh-sah ee Pree-eh-toh of Mexico. Some one hundred and ninety-four thousand people were on the roll, and about eighty-three percent of them turned out. The choice on the ballot was blunt: unite with the Gold Coast, racing towards independence, or remain a United Nations trust territory for now. N-kroo-mah's Convention People's Party campaigned for union; the Togoland Congress, strongest among the southern Eh-weh, campaigned against, dreaming of reunion with the Eh-weh across the French line.

When the count was done, ninety-three thousand and ninety-five people, fifty-eight percent, had chosen union, and sixty-seven thousand, four hundred and ninety-two, forty-two percent, had said no. The map tells it better than the total: the northern districts of Mahm-proo-see, Dah-gom-bah and Gon-jah, together with Boo-em Krah-chee, voted heavily for union, while the two southern Eh-weh districts, Hoh and Kpahn-doo, voted clearly against it. On the thirteenth of December nineteen fifty-six the United Nations General Assembly approved the union, and on the sixth of March nineteen fifty-seven British Togoland was born with Ghana, the long eastern strip running from the Volta up through Oh-tee and Dahg-bong.

## 5. The Palava Desk · 4:35

Now travel north to Baw-koo, near the borders with Burkina Faso and Togo, where the question is not which country but which chief, and there are two histories here; this desk will not pretend there is only one. The Mahm-proo-see say their people founded the Baw-koo skin in the early seventeen hundreds and filled it for two centuries with chiefs sent by the Nah-yee-ree at Nah-leh-ree-goo; the Koo-sah-see say they are the people of the land, its first settlers and its custodians, and that the colonial administration placed Mahm-proo-see chiefs over them, which they date to nineteen thirty-two, because British indirect rule wanted kings to rule through, and the Koo-sah-see had governed themselves without one.

Since independence, government after government has picked up the pen. In nineteen fifty-eight the Court of Appeal ruled for the Koo-sah-see; after the nineteen sixty-six coup a military decree, N. L. C. D. one-one-two, handed the skin to the Mahm-proo-see; in nineteen eighty-three P. N. D. C. Law seventy-five returned it to the Koo-sah-see; and in two thousand and three a Mahm-proo-see suit at the Supreme Court was withdrawn, which each side reads as its own victory. In February twenty twenty-three the Nah-yee-ree en-skinned his own Baw-koo Nah-bah, Al-hah-jee Say-doo Ah-bah-greh, at Nah-leh-ree-goo; when he arrived in Baw-koo in October twenty twenty-four the shooting returned, and by the middle of February twenty twenty-five at least fifty-eight people had been killed, children among them.

Asked to mediate, the Ah-sahn-teh-heh-neh, Oh-toom-fwoh Oh-say Too-too the Second, presented his report on the sixteenth of December twenty twenty-five: it recognised the Koo-sah-see chief, Ah-see-gree Ah-boo-grah-goh Ah-zoh-kah the Second, as Baw-koo Nah-bah, and recommended that Al-hah-jee Ah-bah-greh return to Nah-leh-ree-goo or live in Baw-koo as a private citizen. The government accepted it and promised a one-billion-cedi fund to rebuild Baw-koo; the Nah-yee-ree rejected it entirely as flawed and biased; and on Christmas Day the army moved Al-hah-jee Ah-bah-greh out of Baw-koo. The ten-at-night curfew is still in force, and talks between the two communities, backed by the National Peace Council, go on. Chah-leh, this desk gives no verdict; it only asks you to notice who was holding the pen each time.

## 6. Then vs Now · 6:23

Then, forty-two percent of British Togoland voted to wait; now, a small movement says the wait never ended. On the sixteenth of November twenty nineteen, in Hoh, the Homeland Study Group Foundation, led by Charles Kor-mee Koo-jor-jee, then in his eighties, declared a state of Western Togoland, arguing that the nineteen fifty-six union was never properly formed; and at dawn on the twenty-fifth of September twenty twenty, armed men linked to the movement raided the police stations at Meh-peh and Ah-veh-yee-meh and blocked the roads into the Volta. The state answers that a United Nations plebiscite settled the matter, the Volta chiefs disowned the declaration, and the courts have jailed members of the armed group; yet in May twenty twenty-five the Eh-koh-wahs Court ordered Ghana to pay seventy-five thousand dollars to thirty members held for long periods without trial. Both things are true at once.

It is not only our story: in nineteen forty-seven Cyril Radcliffe, a British lawyer who had never seen India, was given about five weeks to divide it, and millions were uprooted.

And here is the item in the corner of page one. Colonial rule drew lines inside the north too, deciding which chiefs would rule which peoples and writing it into law, so every government since has inherited disputes it could redraw but never erase. It also decided what the north was for: officials sent northern men south to the mines and cocoa farms and kept schools scarce, so the north's first secondary school opened in Tah-mah-leh only in nineteen fifty-one, seventy-five years after Mfahn-tsee-peem in Cape Coast. Eh no be small thing: the north was run as a labour reserve, and Baw-koo still lives inside a decision made for it nearly a century ago.

## 7. The Team Aside · 7:54

Back at the Nest, the hands on that string map belong to Ah-moh, whom the Nest has decided to call The Discerning. He carries the name of Ahn-tohn Vil-helm Ah-moh, a boy taken from near Ak-sim to Germany in the early seventeen hundreds who grew up to teach philosophy there, and like his namesake, our Ah-moh does not take the world as it is handed to him. Ah-moh is blind, and he learns a place by touch; his closest friend at the Nest, whose story is still to come, glued every string down for him, and he traced each line until his fingers knew Yen-dee by the knot where the eighteen ninety-nine string swerves away from it. Late in the evening he peeled the strings off, one by one, and laid his palm flat on the bare card. Kwah-meh, The Combative, who had argued all day about whose line was right, ran his hand over it and went quiet, because under every string the card is one piece. I will tell you more about Ah-moh in good time.

## 8. The Editor's Note · 8:51

Before sound, before security systems, I trained in visual arts, and the first thing any drawing teacher tells you is to sketch in pencil, because pencil can be rubbed out. Every border in this edition was sketched in pencil, in Berlin and London, by men who never walked the ground; our trouble is that we have been inking over them ever since.

## 9. The Letters Page · 9:11

Letters Page, and this week's question asks you to connect: Baw-koo's chiefs, the Volta's separatists and the split of Dahg-bong all trace back to lines drawn before nineteen fourteen, so which colonial line still shapes your own town, your family or your school, and how? Tell us in the comments, and I will pin the best answer.

## 10. The Dispatch · 9:29

That is the third edition of the Gold Coast Pah-lah-vah, complete, checked, stamped, priced at about fifty megabytes, filed and signed at The Nest, two six six Sandpiper Loop, Oh-nee-pah Enclave and dispatched to Accra, to Koo-mah-see, to Boon-kpoo-roo-goo, to Half Ah-see-nee and to the world.
