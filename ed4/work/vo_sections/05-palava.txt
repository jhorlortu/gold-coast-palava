Now the palava your uncle started: is the cedi printed in Switzerland? Here is the record. The Bank of Ghana does not print its notes at home; it buys them from a tiny club of security printers, fewer than twenty in the whole world by the count of its currency head, Dominic Owusu. For years the main printer was De La Rue in Britain, and a former deputy governor, Emmanuel Ah-see-edu Mante, said Ghana had also used firms in Germany and France. The hundred- and two-hundred-cedi notes of twenty nineteen, and the five-cedi note for our sixtieth anniversary, were printed by an American company, Crane Currency, which beat five rivals, among them, yes, a Swiss one, Orell Fussli of Zurich. For the new notes launched this month, the Bank has not publicly named the printer. So there is no public record of the cedi being printed in Switzerland; the nearest Switzerland comes is the ink, because one Swiss company, Sikpa, near Lausanne, supplies the security inks on most of the world's banknotes, though whether that includes ours is not public. But chale, that question is staring at the wrong machine. Most cedis are never printed at all: Ghanaians moved four point five four trillion cedis on mobile money in twenty twenty-five alone, and new money is created whenever the Bank of Ghana lends. In twenty twenty-two it lent the government, through an overdraft, about seven percent of everything the economy produced, created with keystrokes. That year the cedi lost about thirty percent against the dollar, inflation reached fifty-four point one percent in December, so a hundred cedis bought what sixty-five had bought a year earlier, and on the nineteenth of December Ghana stopped paying most of its foreign debts. If you asked "how machay be the plantain?" that year and flinched, you had met monetary policy. Monetary policy, plainly, is the Bank of Ghana setting the price of money. Its policy rate is what banks pay to borrow from it; raise it and loans cost more, spending slows and prices calm down; cut it and money gets cheaper. After twenty twenty-two that rate climbed to thirty percent, the IMF lent three billion dollars under a programme Ghana completed on the twenty-seventh of July this year, inflation fell to five point five percent in October, and on the eighteenth of November the Bank cut its rate from fourteen to eleven percent. And the bill I promised you in our second edition: the IMF says the Bank's gold-purchase programme lost more than one point seven billion dollars in twenty twenty-five, while also crediting it with strengthening the reserves; the Bank of Ghana and GoldBod have defended the programme and disputed how its losses were described. We print both, and you weigh them.
