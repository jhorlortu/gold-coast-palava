Do you remember the gold of our first edition? In Twi, gold and money share one word, see-ka, because for centuries on this land money was gold dust, weighed on small brass scales against little cast figures we now call goldweights, and the smallest weight in that system was the pe-seh-wa, so the coin in your pocket is, by its very name, a pinch of gold. But gold was for big deals, and for buying fish there were cowries, the small white shells the Akan called seh-deh, and they did not come from our beaches: most were gathered in the Maldives, islands in the Indian Ocean, and carried to West Africa in the holds of European ships, often as ballast, in a trade that also bought human beings. Then, in the second half of the eighteen hundreds, merchants shipped in so many cheaper shells from East Africa that the cowrie lost most of its value along the West African coast, and that, my people, is the first inflation in this story: too many shells chasing too few goods. The British replaced shells with shillings. From nineteen twelve a West African Currency Board, sitting in London, issued one currency for Nigeria, the Gold Coast, Sierra Leone and the Gambia, backed by reserves held in London, so the colony's money was only as free as London allowed. On the fourteenth of July nineteen fifty-eight, sixteen months after independence, the new Bank of Ghana issued the Ghana pound, our own notes at last, but still pounds, shillings and pence, until Nkrumah decided that a free country should count in tens.
