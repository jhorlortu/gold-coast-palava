On Monday the nineteenth of July nineteen sixty-five, in the cool, grey middle of the rainy season, when Accra wakes under low cloud and the open gutters still hold the night's rain, Ghanaians queued at bank counters with pound notes in their hands to meet a new money: the cedi, named after the seh-deh, the cowrie, and divided into a hundred pe-seh-was, the old gold weight. One cedi was worth eight shillings and four pence, one Ghana pound bought two cedis and forty pe-seh-was, and on the front of every note was one face, the President's, Kwame Nkrumah's. In the silent film of those queues, people hold the new notes up to the light, as if checking whether freedom had a watermark. Four days after the cedi was born, in Washington, President Lyndon Johnson signed a law taking the silver out of America's dimes and quarters, because the silver had become worth more than the coins it was stamped into: two countries in one week, both admitting that money is a promise, not a metal. Nkrumah's cedi lived nineteen months. After the coup of February nineteen sixty-six, the soldiers of the National Liberation Council issued a New cedi on the seventeenth of February nineteen sixty-seven, one new cedi for one cedi and twenty pe-seh-was, with Nkrumah's face removed; on the ninth of March nineteen seventy-nine another military government changed the notes again and kept thirty percent of the cash you brought to the counter, and half of anything above five thousand cedis; and by two thousand and seven the biggest note, twenty thousand cedis, was worth about two dollars, so on the first of July two thousand and seven four zeros were cut off and ten thousand old cedis became one Ghana cedi.
