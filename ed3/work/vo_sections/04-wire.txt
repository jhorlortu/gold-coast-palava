On Wednesday the ninth of May 1956, in the first weeks of the rainy season, when the farm roads around Ho and Kpahn-doo turn to red mud, the people of British Togoland queued to decide their future under the eye of a United Nations commissioner, Eh-dwar-doh Es-pee-noh-sah ee Pree-eh-toh of Mexico. Some one hundred and ninety-four thousand people were on the roll, and about eighty-three percent of them turned out. The choice on the ballot was blunt: unite with the Gold Coast, racing towards independence, or remain a United Nations trust territory for now. Nkrumah's Convention People's Party campaigned for union; the Togoland Congress, strongest among the southern Ay-vay, campaigned against, dreaming of reunion with the Ay-vay across the French line.

When the count was done, ninety-three thousand and ninety-five people, fifty-eight percent, had chosen union, and sixty-seven thousand, four hundred and ninety-two, forty-two percent, had said no. The map tells it better than the total: the northern districts of Mahm-proo-see, Dah-gom-bah and Gonja, together with Boo-em Krah-chee, voted heavily for union, while the two southern Ay-vay districts, Ho and Kpahn-doo, voted clearly against it. On the thirteenth of December 1956 the United Nations General Assembly approved the union, and on the sixth of March 1957 British Togoland was born with Ghana, the long eastern strip running from the Volta up through Oti and Dahg-bong.
