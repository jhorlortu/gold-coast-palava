Start in Berlin, where from November 1884 to February 1885 the powers of Europe agreed the rules for dividing Africa, with not one African in the room; Germany had already raised its flag east of the Volta that July and called it Togoland. Inland, Britain and Germany both wanted the trading country around Sah-lah-gah and Yendi, so they declared it a neutral zone, which held only until the Germans marched on Yendi, and on the third and fourth of December 1896 the Dahg-bahm-bah fought them at Ah-dee-boh, the battle Dahg-bong still calls Ah-dee-boh dah-lee, the day of Ah-dee-boh, before the Germans burned Yendi to the ground.

Then, on the fourteenth of November 1899, came a deal so strange it belongs on page one: in a treaty mostly about who would keep the islands of Samoa, on the other side of the world, Britain and Germany also split the neutral zone, so Yendi, the seat of the Yah Nah, went to Germany, while Sah-lah-gah and the western part of Dahg-bong stayed British. On the twenty-sixth of September 1901, King Edward the Seventh signed Orders in Council on one day that made Asante a colony, the ending of our first edition, and created the Northern Territories of the Gold Coast, a protectorate in force from the first of January 1902.

The pencil moved again in 1914. Days after Britain declared war on Germany, the Gold Coast Regiment marched into Togoland, and on the seventh of August 1914, near Nwah-chah, a soldier named Alhaji Grunshi returned fire on a German-led police patrol, the first shot fired by a soldier in British service in the whole First World War, less than three weeks before the colony surrendered. Britain and France then cut Togoland in two, and the new line put Yendi back with Dahg-bong on the British side but sliced the Ay-vay people in half.
